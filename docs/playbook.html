1. Purpose and hard rules

1.1 What the application does

News Anchor is the owner's personal, neutral news service on the home server. It:

  1. Collects news continuously from a very large, self-updating registry of verified sources, from free news APIs and from its own crawl of the web. Coverage is thorough at the Global and Country levels (the owner's main use), and also reaches Regions, States and Cities.
  2. Groups articles about the same event into one story and ranks stories so the owner can see the top N (default 10) at any level: any place (Global, a Region such as Asia-Pacific, a Country, a State, a City) combined with any topic (a category, a subcategory or deeper). Neither is required.
  3. Shows every story and article with a link to the original publisher.
  4. Explains every source through a small information icon: how reliable it is, who owns it, what bias is known, what type of source it is, and where each judgement comes from. The trust profile is built by crawling the web for evidence (section 5).
  5. On click, gives a clean AI reading of an article, each point tied to the paragraph it came from.
  6. For any place and topic, writes a briefing that combines several sources per story into a neutral, cited report. The briefing sits in a reading pane that can be resized, minimised and restored.
  7. Pins breaking news first.
  8. Refreshes news, and refreshes the source registry and trust profiles, at intervals the owner sets and on the press of a button.
  9. Puts every setting, including every AI system prompt, on the Settings page, each with its own Reset to default.

3. Where the news comes from

3.1 Coverage priorities (owner's answers)

  • Global and Country levels get the most thorough coverage: every country's main national English-language outlets, wires, public broadcasters, official sources and the international outlets that cover it.
  • Region, State and City levels are covered too, with India, Tamil Nadu and Chennai as the home defaults.
  • English-language sources only by default. Non-English sources are off unless the owner turns on translation (section 7.5), because translation can lose meaning. For countries with few English outlets, coverage comes from English editions of national outlets, international broadcasters and wires.

3.2 Collection layers

The free news APIs are a small extra, not the main source. Their combined free quotas yield at most a few thousand articles a day (apart from the Guardian and NYT, which return only their own articles), mostly 12–24 hours late. 3.5 On-demand backfill

When the owner opens a scope with fewer fresh stories than a threshold (default 10), the page renders what exists at once and the worker runs targeted GDELT, Google News and API queries for that place and topic, then polls the sources that cover that place. New stories stream into the open page. The scope then stays "hot" (kept fresh) for a set time (default 24 h).

3.7 Disk cap and retention (owner-configurable)

The server runs other apps, so News Anchor's disk use is bounded by settings, all on Settings → Storage:

  • Keep articles for N days (default 90, range 3–365). Search reaches back the same distance.
  • Disk cap: the most disk News Anchor may use, in GB. It counts everything the app puts on disk: the database and its write-ahead log and temporary files, backups, the full-text cache, models, logs, and a fixed allowance for its two Docker images (measured at build time and shown as its own line). First run proposes a value and asks the owner to confirm it.
  • Keep at least X GB free on the disk (default 20 GB). This protects the other apps: if they fill the disk, News Anchor gives space back even when it is under its own cap.
  • Separate retention for full text (default 30 days), AI outputs (90 days) and backups (count, default 3; include embeddings, default no).

What Settings → Storage shows. The disk's total and free space (read with statvfs on the data folder, which reports the host disk), News Anchor's use split by part, the measured growth per day over the last 7 days, and two live estimates that update as the owner edits the fields: "N days at the current rate need about X GB" and "your cap holds about N days". A bar shows the cap against the disk's total and free space.

How the limits are enforced (StorageGuard, every 10 minutes and before large writes).

  1. Articles, their tags, embeddings and search index rows are partitioned by day. Removing a day is a DROP of that day's partitions, which frees disk at once (deleting rows in Postgres does not shrink files).
  2. Every day the oldest partitions beyond the retention setting are dropped.
  3. At 90% of the cap, or when free disk is within 2 GB of the floor, the oldest days are dropped early, then the oldest backup, then the full-text cache from oldest, until use is back under 85%. Storage shows "Keeping 74 of 90 days because of the disk cap".
  4. At 100% of the cap, or at the floor, ingestion pauses (no new articles are written) and a banner explains why and links to Storage. Reading, search and AI over stored news keep working.
  5. Postgres limits keep its own spikes inside the cap: max_wal_size 512 MB, temp_file_limit 1 GB, and backups are written to a temporary file that is removed if it would cross the cap.
  6. Lowering the cap or the retention takes effect on the next check; the page shows how much will be removed before the owner saves.

4.3 Source refresh job (default every 7 days, and "Refresh sources now")

  1. Pull changes from Media Cloud, Wikidata, Wikipedia lists and the rating datasets.
  2. Collect new domains and accounts seen since the last run. A domain needs a minimum number of sightings (default 5) before it is considered.
  3. For every candidate: fetch the homepage once, auto-discover feeds (<link rel="alternate">, common paths, robots.txt sitemaps), detect language (only English kept by default), read structured data (schema.org NewsMediaOrganization) and the About, masthead, ethics and corrections pages.
  4. Validate feeds: they parse, have items from the last 14 days, items have real publisher URLs and dates.
  5. Build the trust profile (section 5).
  6. Promote, keep as candidate, or block, with reasons.
  7. Write a refresh report (added, promoted, demoted, retired, with reasons), shown in Settings → Sources → History.

4.6 Searching the web for sources, as built (1 Nov 2026)

Media Cloud and Wikidata only list outlets someone has already catalogued. To find publishers they miss, and to fill the subjects and places the collection covers thinly, the app also searches the news itself:

  • What it asks. Every few hours (setting) it builds a list of questions from the gaps: it counts the stories of the last 7 days for every enabled topic and every country, state and city, and asks first about the ones with the fewest. Subjects are asked with their own words ("(playstation OR ps5)"), places by name, through the news editions of 18 English-speaking markets in rotation (the home country every third time). A question is not repeated within 14 days. Default 30 questions per run, six seconds apart.
  • Where it asks. Google News search results (each result names its publisher and the publisher's address; Google offers the feed for personal readers) and GDELT's document search (worldwide, one request every 5 seconds; when it answers 429 the run carries on without it).
  • What it keeps. Only the publisher, never the articles. Each publisher is counted per question. A publisher that turns up in at least 2 different questions and 3 results, is not already known, and is not an aggregator, press-release wire or platform (MSN, Yahoo, Flipboard, PR Newswire and the like), becomes a candidate with the note "found on the web". Its country comes from its country-code domain only; the search edition says where readers are, not where the publisher is, so otherwise it is "unknown" and its stories are not pinned to a guessed country.
  • Then the same gates as every other candidate: its own feed is found and validated, it is rated by the trust crawler, and its lifecycle is watched. Nothing the search surfaces is collected before its feed has proved itself.

First real run: 30 questions gave 1,101 different publishers, 62 of them new and repeated enough to become candidates; GDELT answered 429 for the whole run from this network and was skipped. Not done: following links out of articles and home pages to discover sites (it would need every article page fetched, and the search-based method already reaches publishers by subject). A bug found on the first run and fixed: the search edition's country was being stored as the publisher's country, which would have filed Reuters' placeless stories under Andorra.

4.4 Feed health job (default daily)

Counts failures, error rates, staleness and redirects; moves feeds between active, degraded and dormant; retries feed discovery for sources whose feeds all died.

5.2 How the score is computed

For each signal i: value v_i in [0,1], weight w_i = base weight × sample factor (0–1, from how much evidence exists).
A neutral prior of 0.5 with weight 1 keeps thin evidence from producing extreme scores.

score      = (1 × 0.5 + Σ w_i × v_i) / (1 + Σ w_i)
confidence = Σ w_i / (Σ w_i + 2)          → low < 0.5 ≤ medium < 0.75 ≤ high

Bands: Highly reliable  score ≥ 0.80 and confidence ≥ medium
       Reliable         score ≥ 0.65
       Mixed            score ≥ 0.45
       Low              score < 0.45
       Unrated          confidence low and no expert signal
Hard rules: deprecated or blacklisted by Wikipedia, or on two or more misinformation lists → blocked.
            3+ false fact-check ratings in 12 months → at most Mixed.

All weights, thresholds and hard rules are settings. Trust profiles are recomputed at every source refresh; fact-check and corroboration signals update daily. A change of band is logged on the source's history.

5.3 Bias profile

  • Source type (N5) and ownership type: public service, state-controlled, party-affiliated, private, non-profit, wire, official body.
  • Owner, from Wikidata, Media Ownership Monitor or the outlet's own disclosure.
  • Political lean only when a named rating set provides it, or when Wikipedia's article on the outlet describes it (cited to that article). Each is shown with its origin.
  • Framing profile measured by News Anchor (phase 6): on stories covered by many outlets, how often this outlet's headlines use loaded words or omit attribution compared with the rest of the coverage. Labelled as our measurement, with the sample size.
  • Country context: the RSF World Press Freedom Index score of the country it operates in, shown as context, not used in the score.

5.4 How it is shown

An information icon next to every source name and on every citation marker opens the card on hover (tap on phones). The card shows type, reliability band with score and confidence, each signal with origin and date, ownership, political lean if rated, fact-check record, corroboration record, and notes such as "paywall". AI-estimated items are labelled. Nothing about reliability is written into the briefing text itself; the citation marker's underline tone shows the band.

When the owner picks a band, a source is shown when its band is at or above it, or when it is unrated and "keep unrated" is on (default on). A story's ranking weight uses scoregamma (gamma 1.5); an unrated source counts as 0.6, so 0.46. Story outlet and coverage counts include only visible sources. Hidden sources are still collected and rated, so lowering the floor shows them at once.

Deviations from the plan in 5.1, and why.

  • JTI and IFCN are not read. The IFCN signatory list is a script-rendered page with no data feed, and the JTI site publishes news posts, not a list of certified outlets. Scraping them would be fragile. They can be added when a machine-readable list exists, or by hand through the override.
  • The Google Fact Check API and misinformation lists are not used, because they need a key or have no stable open list. Fact-check and the "three false ratings caps at Mixed" rule wait for phase 5.
  • Transparency and corroboration can only raise a score. The first real crawl used transparency as a plain 0 to 1 value, and Kotaku and Hindustan Times fell to Mixed because the crawler could not read their footer links (script-built menus). A missing link cannot prove an absent page, so the signal now maps to 0.5–1. That cut the Mixed count from 15 to 5. Corroboration is raise-only for the same reason: a small regional outlet is rarely repeated by others.
  • Not built: corrections behaviour, cited-by-reliable-outlets, originality, look-alike domain and content-farm checks, the AI transparency check (10.6), and the bias profile beyond type, owner and band (5.3). Each is a signal to add later through the same table, with no change to the score formula.

Measured on the 129 starter sources (1 Nov 2026, inside Docker). Highly reliable 21, Reliable 49, Mixed 4 (The Economic Times, Dexerto, Science, The Kyiv Independent), Low 1 (MercoPress), Unrated 54. With the defaults of that time, 5 sources were hidden; since v0.7 none are. The crawl took 125 seconds. Right after it the app container used about 97 MiB and the database about 57 MiB.

Reading the results with care.

  • The 54 unrated sources are mostly official bodies, company publications and specialist sites (for example F1 and cricket publications) that the Lin set and Wikipedia do not cover. Unrated is not unreliable; they stay visible by default and show their type on every card.
  • The Lin dataset leans towards US and European outlets, so Indian regional outlets are more often unrated than rated.
  • Science, The Kyiv Independent and The Economic Times are placed Mixed on one dataset each. That is the evidence the crawler has; the card shows it, and the override is there for a judgement the owner disagrees with.
  • Wikipedia and the Lin set are not neutral authorities; they are the best open evidence available and are named as such on every card.

Screens. The source card (hover or tap) shows the band, score, confidence, owner and each piece of evidence with a link to its origin. The Sources page lists all sources with a band filter, the evidence table, an override control, "Refresh trust now" with progress, and a "How trust is worked out" section with the live weights. Story strips tone each segment by band; the story view lists "Other coverage" from lower-rated sources when that setting is on.

What changed.

  1. One rating set alone never calls a source Low. A verdict that harsh needs two independent sets to agree; with one it is Mixed, and the card says "based on one expert rating set". In the test this removed every false Low (4 of 126) at the price of more unreliable sources being called Mixed rather than Low (47 instead of 38).
  2. Disagreement is shown, not averaged away. When two sets differ by 0.5 or more the card says so.
  3. A band changes only when the score is clearly past a threshold (margin 0.02, a setting), so noise in a weak signal cannot flip a source back and forth. Every change is in the source's history.
  4. Nothing is hidden by default. Owner's decision: the earlier floor that hid Mixed, Low and blocked sources is now an optional view filter, off by default ("Hide sources rated below…"). A bad rating therefore cannot make a source disappear. The band changes the label, the ranking weight and the story's verification mark, nothing more. The owner can override any band, and the override is logged.
  5. Each story carries a verification mark computed from the outlets behind it: Confirmed (two or more reliable outlets), Reported (two or more outlets, fewer than two reliable), One reliable outlet, or "Check before sharing" (one source that is not rated reliable, or only Mixed or lower sources). This is the guard against a false story looking legitimate: it never says a story is true, only how many reliable outlets stand behind it. The AI digest and briefings also show it.

What is deliberately not done. Plagiarism and AI-written-text detection are not scored, and will not be. Detectors for AI-written text are known to misclassify human writing, and a "plagiarised" tag on a publisher's record that is wrong is exactly the harm the owner asked to avoid. Plagiarism between outlets can be seen where it is real (the same text under two bylines) as an observation on a story, not as a mark on a source; that is not built. JTI, IFCN and the misinformation lists are still not used (no stable machine-readable list).

Limits that remain. The test is against two expert sources, not against truth. About half the sources the registry finds have no expert rating at all and stay unrated: Lin's set leans towards US and European outlets, so outlets from Africa, Latin America, South and Southeast Asia are rated less often. "Unrated" is not a verdict, and the verification mark counts it as "not rated reliable" for a reason: you are told that nothing vouches for it, not that it is false.

6. Ingestion pipeline (news refresh)

6.1 Steps

Default every 15 minutes (5–240), plus "Refresh news now". Each feed also has its own adaptive interval.

  1. Fetch due feeds concurrently (default 8 in the Light profile, at least 2 s between requests to one host), with conditional GETs.
  2. Parse RSS/Atom/sitemaps/API responses.
  3. Resolve the publisher URL (redirects, canonical URL, tracking parameters stripped, Google News links decoded only when needed).
  4. De-duplicate: URL hash, near-duplicate titles (SimHash), syndicated wire copy (counts once for ranking).
  5. Language check (fastText language ID): non-English dropped unless translation is on.
  6. Embed title and lead with a small English sentence-embedding model, 384 dimensions, on the CPU through ONNX (candidates: bge-small-en-v1.5, e5-small-v2, snowflake-arctic-embed-s; picked by a benchmark on our own data in phase 3). The same embedding serves clustering and semantic search.
  7. Classify topics (7.3), geotag (7.4), detect breaking news (8.2).
  8. Cluster into stories (online, 72-hour window, embeddings plus shared named entities).
  9. Score stories (section 8), invalidate affected scope caches, notify open pages.

6.2 Full text: fetch and extract

Full text is fetched when an article is opened or needed for a digest, and cached for 30 days. Article briefs and digests always link the original. The extractor (dao/extract/article.py) runs these steps and stops when it has the complete article:

  1. Session, if provided. If Settings → Sources → Sessions holds cookies or a bearer token for this outlet (owner-supplied, encrypted, none today), they are attached to the request.
  2. Standard request. One GET with a standard browser User-Agent and Accept-Language, following redirects. Responses are cached so a page is never fetched twice.
  3. Look everywhere the page already carries the text. The raw HTML and its scripts are inspected and every candidate is collected:
    • the visible article markup (<article>, main content);
    • schema.org JSON-LD NewsArticle.articleBody;
    • embedded page state used by common frameworks: __NEXT_DATA__ (Next.js), window.__INITIAL_STATE__ / __PRELOADED_STATE__, __NUXT__, Fusion.globalContent (Arc XP), and similar JSON in <script> tags;
    • sections the publisher's own paywall markup (isAccessibleForFree=false with a hasPart cssSelector) marks as gated but which are present in the HTML and only hidden with CSS;
    • the AMP version (link rel="amphtml") when it carries more text.
    The longest candidate that passes quality checks wins: enough paragraphs, low link density, its opening matches the title or lead, no subscription-widget text.
  4. Render if the text is loaded by script. If no candidate has the article (the page fills it in with JavaScript after loading), the page is opened once in headless Chromium (Playwright) and step 3 runs again on the rendered page. Chromium is the heaviest part of the app (about 300–500 MB while open), so it starts only when needed, renders one page, and is closed at once; it is skipped, and the brief falls back to step 6, when the server has less free memory than a set floor or the RAM budget would be exceeded. It can be switched off.
  5. Clean text extraction. rs-trafilatura (best word-level F1 on the 2,008-page WCXB benchmark, 0.859, at about 44 ms a page) with trafilatura (0.791) as the fallback. It strips navigation, sidebars, ads, scripts, related-story blocks and cookie banners and returns the article's paragraphs, which are numbered p1…pn for citations. A cleanup pass then removes interface text that extractors keep by mistake (a phrase list plus per-outlet CSS selectors to drop, editable in Settings).
  6. Only a preview? If the publisher's server sends only a preview, the app uses an existing public Internet Archive snapshot when one exists (linked next to the original), then the story's other coverage. The brief's coverage note says which.

6.3 Paywalls

Steps 1–6 above are the paywall handling the owner asked for, minus one technique: Googlebot mimicry is not built. Sending Google's crawler name to get text a publisher shows only to Google for indexing is impersonation to get around the paywall, which the app does not do (N9). It also fails where it matters: large publishers verify Googlebot by reverse DNS, as Google documents, and fake-Googlebot requests from a home IP get that IP blocked, which would also break the normal crawl of that outlet.

Test on 30 Oct 2026. 22 articles from today's feeds (The Hindu, Mint, The Indian Express, BBC, The Guardian, Al Jazeera) were fetched with step 2 alone. All returned HTTP 200 with the article text in the page. The Hindu and Mint mark some of them "not free" in their structured data. In a Mint premium explainer, JSON-LD articleBody held only 63 words, but the page HTML held the whole article (about 840 words), so step 3 recovers it. A Hindu article came back with its subscription widget text appended ("You don't have any Active Subscription…"), which is why step 5 has a cleanup pass. One Hindu explainer was short (about 190 words); the test could not tell whether that was the whole piece. JSON-LD bodies were absent on BBC, Guardian and Al Jazeera pages, where the visible markup carried the full text.

Paywalled sources stay in lists with headline and link and a "Paywall" mark.

6.4 Reading an article here, as built

Only when the owner opens an article in the reading pane (never in bulk): the page as a browser would get it (the publisher's robots.txt obeyed while Obey robots.txt rules is on), cleaned with trafilatura; if that gives little, the article text the publisher declares in its own structured data or embedded page data, then the AMP version; if the page is blocked, the Internet Archive's own public copy, labelled as such. Results are cached by address. When only a summary is available the pane and the AI reading both say so. There is no search-engine impersonation, login or paywall removal, and no headless browser: pages that need a script engine to show any text come back as "summary only". A headless renderer (Chromium, about 300 to 500 MB while running) is the one planned piece of 6.2 that is not built, because of the memory it costs on a shared server.

8.4 Search (semantic, with exact-name matching)

The search box (/ or Ctrl/⌘ K, then type a sentence) finds stored articles from the last 90 days by meaning. Results are grouped by story and can be filtered by place, topic, date range, source type and reliability band; the current scope is applied by default and can be cleared with one click.

Why hybrid. The owner asked for semantic search. Pure semantic search finds "arrest of former Tamil Nadu education minister" when the headline says "Anbil Mahesh Poyyamozhi arrested", but it is weak on exact identifiers that carry no meaning to an embedding model: "GSLV-F18", "NVS-03", "KNG22", an unusual surname. Published comparisons show adding keyword matching to vector search raises retrieval precision noticeably (one report: from about 62% to 84%). So the app runs both and fuses them, with semantic weighted higher by default.

  1. The query is embedded on the CPU with the same model as the articles (a few milliseconds).
  2. Semantic: the HNSW index on 1-bit (binary-quantised) vectors returns the 200 nearest articles by Hamming distance; they are re-ranked by exact cosine distance on the stored half-precision vectors. Binary quantisation keeps the index small (about 2 GB at the expected volume, on disk) at a small recall cost that the re-ranking mostly recovers. The index is read through the operating system's file cache, which Linux gives back to other apps when they need memory.
  3. Lexical: Postgres full-text search on title and lead (websearch_to_tsquery, so "quotes", OR and -minus work), plus trigram matching for names and typos.
  4. Fusion: Reciprocal Rank Fusion, score = Σ weight_m / (60 + rank_m), semantic weight 1.0 and lexical 0.6 by default.
  5. Optional re-rank: a small cross-encoder (about 90 MB) re-scores the top 50 on the CPU. Off in the Light profile; loaded only for a search and unloaded after 5 minutes idle in the others.
  6. Results are grouped by story, show the matching sentence of the lead, and open in the reading pane like any story.

Targets in the Light profile, measured in phase 3: under 1 second for the last 7 days (the default range), a few seconds for all 90 days, because older days are read from the SSD rather than memory.

9.2 Routing

  • Per task, an ordered chain: long inputs (digests, briefings) prefer long-context providers; short tasks (labels, translation) prefer fast ones. Chains are editable.
  • The router reads rate-limit headers and 429s, keeps a per-provider counter that resets on each provider's own schedule, and moves on immediately when a provider refuses. SDK retries are off.
  • Every output records provider, model and prompt version, shown in small text under it.
  • A usage page shows calls and tokens per provider today and over 30 days.

9.3 Budget and caching

  • Article brief: one call, cached per article and prompt version.
  • Story digest: one call per story, regenerated only when the story gains a significant source. Shared by every scope containing the story.
  • Briefing: mostly cached digests plus one combining call.
  • Background briefings (owner: acceptable, as a toggle): for Global and India, all topics and each top-level category, plus favourites. A briefing is regenerated only when its top stories change materially, at most once per interval (default 60 min). Rough load: a few hundred to about 600 calls a day across the chain. "On demand only" is one switch.

10. AI system prompts

All prompts below are defaults. Each is editable in Settings → AI → Prompts with: the text, the list of variables it receives, a read-only view of the output schema (always appended by the app, so an edit cannot break rendering or citation checks), "Test on a sample" (runs it on a chosen article or story and shows the result), a diff against the default, and Reset to default. Saving a prompt gives it a new version; cached outputs from older versions are regenerated when next opened.

10.1 Shared rules (prepended to every prompt)

You work for one reader as a neutral news editor. Your job is to tell the reader what is happening,
clearly and fairly, using only the material provided in this request.

Rules:
1. Use only the provided material. Do not add facts, names, titles, numbers or background from memory.
   If something the reader would need is not in the material, say it is not covered.
2. Every factual statement must cite the input it comes from, using only the ids given
   (paragraph ids like p3, or source ids like S2). Never invent an id. Never write a URL.
3. Attribute claims. Allegations, estimates, predictions, characterisations and disputed numbers are
   written as "X said / according to X", naming who said it.
4. Name the type of source whenever you rely on an official or social source: "the Tamil Nadu
   government said in a press release", "in a post on Bluesky, X said". Never present a social post
   as established fact.
5. Keep the original uncertainty: "reportedly", "alleged", "preliminary", "unconfirmed" stay.
6. Separate fact, claim and opinion. If a piece is opinion, analysis or a press release, say so.
7. Do not take sides, do not use judging adjectives (shocking, historic, controversial) unless quoting
   someone, and do not tell the reader what to think.
8. Convert relative dates to absolute dates using the publication time given. Keep units and currencies.
9. Plain English for an informed adult. Short sentences. No filler, no hype.
10. If the material is too thin for a section, leave that section empty rather than padding it.
11. Output only JSON that matches the schema.

11.2 Processes (Docker Compose: two containers)

  • app: one Python process that serves the UI and API and runs the scheduler and jobs on background threads (crawling, APIs, embedding, clustering, scoring, trust crawler, AI calls). One process instead of separate web and worker processes saves about 100–150 MB. Heavy steps run at lower CPU priority so the API stays responsive. The same image can still run as separate web and worker processes (NA_ROLE=web|worker) if that is ever needed.
  • db: PostgreSQL with pgvector, data in ./data/postgres; nightly pg_dump to ./data/backups.

Manual refreshes are rows in a jobs table whose progress the page polls and also receives as server-sent events from an in-process bus. A single scheduler loop inside the app process starts a cycle when feeds are due (checked every 30 seconds). Story assignment is one writer at a time, because two feeds writing at once would each create their own story for the same event.

Settings → Resources (all owner-configurable):

  • Profile: Light (default), Balanced or Fast. It sets the values below together; any value can then be changed on its own and reset.
  • RAM budget for News Anchor (default 1 GB in Light). The ResourceGovernor reads the app's real use every few seconds and stays under the budget by lowering fetch concurrency, unloading models, and skipping Chromium and re-ranking.

  • Fetch concurrency, model keep-alive time, Chromium on or off, re-rank on or off, Postgres work_mem.
  • The page shows the server's total and available RAM, News Anchor's use split into app and database, and the peak over the last 24 hours.

How the numbers are read and applied. Server total and available RAM come from /proc/meminfo. The app reads its own container's use from its cgroup. The db container runs a small loop that copies its cgroup memory figure to a shared file every 30 seconds, so the app can show it without any access to Docker. Postgres memory settings that can be changed live (work_mem, maintenance_work_mem, autovacuum) are applied with ALTER SYSTEM and a reload, on parameters granted to the app's database role; shared_buffers stays fixed at 128 MB because the design does not depend on it. Each container also has a hard memory limit in .env (defaults: app 1.5 GB, db 512 MB) as a backstop the governor should never reach; the page shows those limits and says to edit .env to change them, because the app has no access to Docker by design.

Trade-off of the Light profile: refreshes take a little longer with fewer parallel fetches, and searches over older days read from the SSD. Neither affects the news list, which stays under 300 ms because it reads small, recent, indexed tables.

The GPU is not used: embedding 80,000–150,000 short texts a day takes minutes on the CPU.

20.3 The organic discovery pipeline

Five finders run side by side on their own schedules. Each publisher they find becomes a candidate whose feed is then found and validated like any other source, and the trust crawler rates it.

  • Stream (GDELT). Every hour, the quarter-hour files not read yet (up to 4, English and translated). A publisher is promoted after it appears in 3 separate quarter hours with at least 3 articles. First run: 533 publishers in 44 languages from 6 files.
  • Editions (Google News). Since v0.9.2: the 9 section web pages of each edition in a collected language (198 pages for the 22 English editions), 30 pages an hour, so a full sweep takes about 7 hours. Local pages are no longer read (20.14).
  • Wikidata per country. 10 countries an hour, those with the fewest sources first; each country again after 30 days. First run: 10 countries, 99 outlets.
  • Directory (Media Cloud, Wikidata feeds). Weekly. All languages are now admitted. The read is resumable: the collections already read are remembered, so a restart continues where it stopped instead of starting over.
  • Web search. News searches for the subjects and places covered thinnest. GDELT's DOC API since v0.9.2. Google News search before that, which robots.txt disallows (20.14).

Where a publisher is. In order: its country-code domain; the one country whose national or local Google News pages carry it; the country where at least 60% of the events it reports take place (GDELT); the country a search service named; otherwise unknown (treated as international). The search edition alone is never used, because it says where readers are, not where the publisher is.

Balance. Each country has a target (30 sources) and a limit (200). Countries under the target are served first at every step: promotion of new publishers, the order of feed checks, and which countries Wikidata is asked about. A country at the limit takes no new sources. Parking (stopping the weakest discovered sources of an over-full country) exists but is off by default: on its first run it parked 788 working US sources, which removed real local coverage without improving ranking (independent-outlet counting already stops syndication from dominating, 20.5). They were all brought back automatically, and parking is now an opt-in setting.

How long discovery takes. It never finishes, by design: every finder repeats. On this server: the Media Cloud directory pass takes about 3.4 hours for its 1,527 collections and repeats weekly; the editions sweep takes about 16 hours; Wikidata reaches every under-covered country in about 23 hours; the GDELT stream and feed checks (about 60 candidates a minute) run continuously. Most growth happens in the first two days.

20.4 Languages and translation

  • All languages are collected by default (Settings → Sources). The language of each headline is detected without a model, from its writing system and common short words, with the publisher's usual language settling shared scripts. A detection model (py3langid) was rejected: about 130 MB more in the app process.
  • Headlines are translated into English on this server with free OPUS-MT models packaged by Argos, run with CTranslate2 (int8) in a child process that exits between batches. Measured: 40 headlines in 0.57 s, about 320 MB while running. A language's model (66 to 285 MB) is downloaded once it has 10 articles in two days; models stay inside a 3,000 MB disk budget, least recently used first out. Models come with either a SentencePiece or a BPE tokenizer; both are handled.
  • Only English text or an English translation gets a meaning vector: the English model gave a meaningless 0.92 similarity between unrelated Greek and Bengali headlines. Switching to a multilingual model was rejected for memory.
  • Translated headlines show "Translated from …" with the original on hover and in the story view.
  • At the time of writing: 63 languages among sources, 25 translation models installed (2.6 GB), 7,635 headlines translated.

20.5 Story clustering, independence and ranking

Calibration. 210 story pairs from the live data were labelled by hand (108 the same event). The old rule had precision 0.92 and recall 0.10, which is why one event, such as the Binance hack, appeared as a dozen stories.

  • New rule: merge when cosine similarity ≥ 0.80, or ≥ 0.65 and the headlines share rare words (IDF mass ≥ 0.10 of the smaller headline).
  • Never merge: routine items; two different cities or states named; different casualty counts. Groups join only while their average vectors stay close (≥ 0.62), which stops chains.
  • Result on the labelled pairs: precision 0.92, recall 0.86, F1 0.89.
  • Rejected: a general numbers guard ("$3bn" against "$6.3bn" blocked true merges) and a capitalised-word entity guard (it failed on Title Case headlines).

Wire copy. Top was dominated by AP copy reprinted by about 98 US local TV sites. Each article now has a fingerprint of its headline and opening; outlets printing the same text count once. "Independent outlets" replaces "outlets" in ranking, Top eligibility, Trending and Breaking. Coverage is the sum of the best source weight per fingerprint.

Routine items. Scoreboards, schedules, bulletins, obituaries, horoscopes, dated photo galleries and local weather forecasts are listed in Latest and in their place and topic, but never ranked in Top or Trending, and never merged by wording. Before this rule, one "story" held 114 local forecasts from 79 stations.

One full re-cluster after the upgrade folded 13,348 duplicate stories in the 48-hour window (53,560 stories). It first ran out of memory at 1.5 GB because the vectors were read as text; reading them streamed, as numbers, peaked at 814 MB. After it, Top led with the Binance hack: 175 independent outlets, 335 outlets in all.

Definitions. Top: stories updated in the last 24 hours with at least 2 independent outlets (single-source stories fill thin lists, marked), not social-only, not routine, ranked by coverage × country-diversity boost × 0.5^(hours since last update ÷ 8). Breaking: at least 5 independent non-social outlets within 30 minutes of the first publication; the red mark lasts 3 hours. All of these numbers are settings.

20.6 Places and topics

  • Places now cover the world: 3,864 states and provinces and 31,781 cities of 15,000 people or more (GeoNames, CC BY 4.0), with India's curated codes kept. A name with several candidates is decided by the state or country named alongside it, by a dominant big city, and by the publisher's country. Common words, first names, lowercase words and prefixes ("Lake Geneva") are skipped. "Salem, Massachusetts" is no longer placed in India.
  • A city paper's fixed place (a Chennai paper's feed is tagged Tamil Nadu) now applies only to articles that name no place or only places inside it, so its national sport and world news are no longer Tamil Nadu news.

20.11 Fair Top, Trending and Breaking; developments and threads (v0.9)

Research. Three established ideas fit the problem. Topic Detection and Tracking (the DARPA TDT programme, Allan et al.) separates an event (something that happens at a time and place) from a topic (the storyline it belongs to), and treats "first story detection" as finding the first report of a new event. Kleinberg's burst detection (2002) models a stream's rate and calls a sharp rise against its own normal rate a burst. Google News documents its ranking signals as relevance, prominence, authoritativeness, freshness, location and language, and aims to show diverse perspectives. Nothing published describes a ranking that corrects for an unbalanced source list; that part is ours.

What was wrong. 48 of the then Top 50 stories spanned more than a day of coverage, and half of each story's coverage was more than a day old. A "story" was really a storyline, and any new report kept its oldest event at the top. Ranking summed reliability-weighted outlets, so 175 US outlets beat 30 countries.

  • Developments and threads. A development is the reports of one event within 24 hours of its first report (setting). A report about the same storyline later than that starts a new development in the same thread. The merger merges similar stories only within the window and joins threads beyond it, using the same calibrated rule (precision 0.92).
  • Fair coverage. Each wire-copy group counts once and is credited to its best-weighted outlet's country. Each country contributes log2(1 + its weighted independent outlets), and the contributions are summed. So 5 outlets in one country score 1.7 and one outlet in each of 5 countries scores 2.7 (unrated weights). Social posts earn nothing.
  • Top · Fresh: developments first reported in the last 24 hours, by fair coverage halving every 8 hours since the first report; Breaking first. Top · Ongoing: threads that began more than 24 hours ago and have new developments in the last 24 hours, by the fair coverage of those developments; each row shows the newest development carried by at least 2 outlets.
  • Trending · Fresh: new developments by how many outlets published them in the last 2 hours, counted per country with the same log rule. Trending · Ongoing: a burst. Outlets in the last 2 hours against the thread's own rate over the 24 hours before: z = (recent − expected) / √(expected + 1). Shown when z > 1 and at least 3 outlets.
  • Breaking: at least 5 independent non-social outlets, one per source and wire copy once, within 30 minutes of the first report. Two bugs fixed: Breaking stories had sorted below every unmarked story (NULL ordering), and one source's several items counted as several outlets. From 8 Nov 2026, three or more outlets stamped with the very same publication instant count as one report: a scheduled syndicated column (five Advance Local sites carried one Mayo Clinic column at 14:00:00) had been marked Breaking. Checked against the last three days of Breaking stories: only the two syndicated ones fell below the threshold, real news was unchanged.
  • When the event is. Each row says "First reported Sun 1 Nov, 15:30", "Ongoing since Thu 29 Oct · 4 developments", or "Scheduled: Tue 3 Nov (in 2 days)". Scheduled needs a forward cue and a named day, agreed by at least two outlets and a third of them. A general date parser was rejected: it found dates in 9 of 40 real headlines, several wrong. The story view shows the thread's timeline and the coverage grouped by day, newest first. The AI digest now also states when the event happens, with a citation.
  • One-time migration: 1,563 long stories were cut into 1,764 more developments, then re-clustered (908 merges, 326 thread joins).

Limits. The thread joining uses the same-event rule, so it under-joins rather than over-joins. A storyline can split into two threads when its events are worded differently. Fairness corrects for how many sources a country has, not for which countries have none.

Why MotoGP and Formula 1 shared a storyline. Headline-word matching put "Hamilton wins pole for Ferrari at the Mexico City Grand Prix" in the MotoGP sprint story: the two share "win", "pole", "grand", "prix". The story then held 95 MotoGP and 126 F1 reports. A storyline link (single link, no check against the rest of the storyline) and the one-off split into developments did the rest. The fix follows news-stream clustering research (Miranda et al. 2018; Saravanakumar et al. 2021; Story Forest, Liu et al. 2017), which compares the people, organisations and places a report names, not only its wording (services/anchors.py):

  • A headline's names are its capitalised words that are not everyday words (measured on 100,736 headlines: a word written in lower case a quarter of the time is everyday), codes with digits (F1, G7) and acronyms; places and demonyms count as their country; name-like words many events share (Grand Prix, Cup, Senate) do not count.
  • Two reports are different events when each names two or more things, none shared, and they share too few rare words (one if either names only two things, two otherwise). This veto applies to joining a story at ingest, to merging by meaning, and to storylines. On 360 sampled pairs from the live data it separated 18, 17 rightly.
  • Developments days apart join one storyline only with positive evidence (a shared name, a shared rare word, or the same headline) and only if they do not name other things than the storyline's first development. Merging two stories carries a storyline across only under the same test.
  • The stored data was repaired: reports that named other things than their story's headline and most of its reports left it (1,673 reports, 692 new stories), and developments unrelated to their storyline left it (644). The F1 and MotoGP storylines are now separate.

How long a storyline lasts. Nothing ends a storyline on a timer. A report joins a storyline when it matches one of its developments updated in the last 72 hours (headline words) or 48 hours (meaning). After a quiet spell longer than that, the next report starts a new storyline. A development is one day's event (setting Feed › One development lasts, 24 hours). Stories are deleted only with their articles, after the retention period (90 days) or when the disk cap needs room.

The Storylines tab ranks storylines by the coverage their developments drew in a chosen period: 6 or 12 hours, 1, 2, 3, 7, 14 or 30 days (setting Lists › Storylines tab: period). The score is the sum, over developments with a report in the period, of each development's fair coverage (each text counted once, credited by country with diminishing returns, as in Top). A storyline needs the "Outlets needed for Top" in the period. Each card shows the development with the most coverage, the latest one, outlets and countries in the period, a bar per slice of the period (24 for up to 3 days) and the last five developments. A minimum number of developments is a setting (default 1). The request takes about 0.3 s, cached for a minute.

The reader. On 120 random articles: full text 78 before, 81 after; opening only 13 and 12; publisher's summary only 29 and 23 (4 more timed out in the test harness). Changes: a post that shares an article is read at the linked article ("Links to ..." was the post itself); News Anchor's own agent first and, if refused or thin, once more as a browser-compatible reader that still names itself (setting Search › Open articles as a browser-compatible reader; several sites refuse non-browser agents with HTTP 403 or 406, while others answer a browser agent with a script challenge, HTTP 202, so the order matters); article text from Arc XP page data and other block lists; trafilatura's wider pass when the careful one finds little, kept only if it reads as prose (a Lee Enterprises page ships the subscriber text scrambled; it is not decoded); a failed read is retried after 6 hours instead of never; feed items that are image or video files are skipped. Not worked around: paywalls and logins, bot walls that refuse every agent (The Economist, Bloomberg, Forbes, The New York Times, and HTTP 429 from TownNews sites), and pages built only by script (a headless browser would read those, at 200 to 400 MB a page). The AI story digest now tries up to six reports to find three with text.

Trust for sources without an expert rating. Research consulted: TrustRank (Gyöngyi et al. 2004) spreads trust from expert-reviewed sites along links; Fairbanks et al. 2018 found the link structure predicts reliability better than article text; Baly et al. 2018 learn experts' labels from features any site has; Burdisso et al. 2024 learn reliability from the news hyperlink graph (Spearman 0.80 against journalists' labels) and publish estimates for 15,425 domains; Carragher et al. 2024 classify and discover unreliable sites from link neighbourhoods; truth discovery (Yin et al. 2007) rates sources by how often others confirm them. Built:

  • Evidence gathered: fact-checkers' labels for 5,332 domains from Burdisso et al.'s dataset (MBFC, NewsGuard, Wikipedia; an expert signal, used where Lin et al. has no score: 129 sources gained a rating); the published hyperlink-graph estimate (2,076 sources); citations, meaning links in feed items and article pages, and credits such as "according to Tasnim" in rated outlets' headlines (table source_links, collected from now on); sister outlets with the same owner (209); pickup by Reliable or better outlets in their own words (989).
  • A model trained daily on the sources experts rated (services/trust_model.py: logistic regression, five-fold cross-validation) gives each other source the chance experts would call it Reliable. A band is estimated only where the model is right for at least 85% (Reliable) or 80% (Mixed) of rated sources on that side of the cut; otherwise the source stays Unrated. Estimated bands are marked "(estimated)", never Highly reliable or Low, and never count towards Confirmed, since part of the evidence is pickup by rated outlets.
  • Today the model reaches a cross-validated AUC of 0.71 and no cut is that precise, so no source has an estimated band yet; the evidence is shown on each source card. A first version that also used transparency pages and domain age reached 0.75, but marked every small local outlet Mixed: experts mostly rate large outlets, so the model learned to mark outlets down for looking small. Those two signals were taken out of the model. As citations accumulate the model is retrained daily, and estimated bands appear once they are precise enough.

Ranking, restated. Top · Fresh: developments first reported within the window (24 h), by fair coverage halved every 8 hours since the first report; Breaking first, then those with 2 or more independent outlets, then single-source ones. Top · Ongoing: storylines that began more than a day ago and have something new in the window, by the fair coverage of their recent developments, each halved since its last report, shown by the newest development with several outlets. Trending · Fresh: developments first reported within the window, by outlets in the last 2 hours, counted by country with diminishing returns. Trending · Ongoing: storylines whose outlets in the last 2 hours are well above their rate over the day before (z above 1).

20.17 Trust weights learned from the evidence, geographic focus, headlines chosen from the reports (v0.9.5)

Why the Economic Times was Mixed. Its expert score came from Lin et al. 2023's aggregate, 0.22 at weight 3. The per-rater file shows that, of the five public rating sets in it, only two actually rated the site: the Lewandowsky et al. list (0) and the MisinfoMe list (listed). The other values are imputed. Across the whole file, 6,105 of 11,520 domains have no real public rating behind their aggregate (whale.to scores 0.77 although its one real rating lists it as misinformation). Media Bias/Fact Check itself now rates the Economic Times Questionable, citing failed fact checks. So the low verdict is not only a weighting artefact, but it was counted with more certainty than the evidence carries.

  • Lin et al.'s score now counts in proportion to the public sets that really rated a site: 25% with none, 70% with one, 100% with two or more. The source card names them.
  • Fact-checkers' labels (MBFC, NewsGuard, Wikipedia, via Burdisso et al.) count for every source, unless the same verdict is already counted (Wikipedia's when the perennial-sources signal exists; MBFC's when Lin et al. has a real MBFC rating).
  • Weights are learned daily (setting Trust › Learn the weights from the evidence). A one-factor model, as in truth discovery (Li et al. 2014, CRH) and in Lin et al.'s own principal component, treats each signal as the source's quality plus noise. Each signal's reliability is the share of its spread it shares with the others (from triads of covariances). Its weight is reliability/(1 − reliability), capped at 3, shrunk toward the starting weight by (n·learned + 200·start)/(n + 200). Measured evidence is first mapped onto the experts' scale by least squares on the rated sources.
  • Learned on 6,937 sources: Wikipedia's list 3.0 (95% shared), hyperlink-graph estimate 1.95 (67%), Lin et al. 1.19 (33%), fact-checkers' labels 0.97 (21%), citations by rated outlets 1.26 (38 sources only, so mostly its starting weight), sister outlets 0.49, pickup by rated outlets 0.16, transparency pages 0.09, ownership 0.09, domain age 0.04. The last four barely agree with any expert verdict, so they hardly count now.
  • Why the measured evidence had weight 0 before: it was kept out of rated sources' scores so that only expert ratings decided their band, and fed only the estimate for unrated sources. It now counts by its measured agreement. The hyperlink graph was built from English-language news, so it counts half for sites in other languages.
  • The estimate model for unrated sources now learns from the experts' verdict alone. A first run learned from bands that already included the measured evidence and reported a meaningless AUC of 0.95. Now: AUC 0.80, 181 sources estimated Reliable (85% precision on rated sources) and 61 Mixed (80%).
  • Results: Economic Times 0.48 → 0.55, still Mixed. Mint Mixed → Reliable. Times of India Reliable → Mixed (Wikipedia's "no consensus" counts fully, and only one public set rated it). The Hindu 0.75 → 0.77. Expert-rated: Highly reliable 48 → 60, Reliable 649 → 731, Mixed 476 → 468, Low 15 → 11.

Places a report is about, not every place it names. A report on Indian bond yields that compared them with US Treasury yields was tagged United States and listed under North America. Research on geographic focus (NLGF, Ariyarathne et al. 2026; Web-a-Where, Amitay et al. 2004; Cliff-Clavin) scores each place by where it is mentioned and keeps the main ones. Adapted to headline and summary (Gazetteer.detect_focus):

  • Each country, with its states and cities, scores 3 for the headline's first clause, 1 for a later clause, 1.5 for a dateline ("MUMBAI:", "New Delhi [India], Nov 1 (ANI):"), and 1 or 0.5 for the summary's first or later sentences. A mention after a word of comparison or context (vs, than, cues from, amid, despite, ahead of ...) counts half. The publisher's own country adds 0.5. The publisher name some feeds append ("- Telegraph India") is ignored.
  • A place is kept if it has more than 25% of the total, or more than half of the top place's score.
  • Institutions and markets now name their country: RBI, Sensex and SEBI for India; Wall Street, the Federal Reserve, the White House and Nasdaq for the US; and others.
  • On 117 hand-labelled reports naming two or more countries, precision of country tags rose from 77% to 89%; 90% of the right ones were kept. The weights were tuned on the same sample, so expect a little less on new data.
  • The Indian bond story is now tagged India in 6 of its 6 reports and the US in 1, so it is no longer in North America. A story still counts for a place when half its reports are about it, so a two-report story where one report names the US is still listed there.

Headlines. A story kept the first report's headline, and the latest report alone is no better. Each story's headline is now chosen from its reports (services/headlines.py; setting Lists › Choose each story's headline from its reports), the way centroid summarisation chooses sentences (MEAD, Radev et al. 2004):

  • A headline scores by how much of its rare-word mass the story's reports share.
  • Each report votes with a weight that halves every 12 hours before the latest report and grows with its outlet's reliability weight.
  • Questions, live blogs, "watch" and "photos" items, all-capital headlines, and very short or very long ones count less. Social posts never title a story.
  • The line under it is that report's own summary, or else a summary from a report saying much the same.
  • Storylines get a title the same way from all their reports (half-life 24 hours). The Storylines tab shows it, with the latest development below.
  • The headline is always one an outlet published, and the card credits that outlet. Its tooltip says who reported first.
  • On the first run, 6,358 of 8,206 stories took another report's headline and 1,800 storylines got titles. For example, "Browns go for first sweep of Steelers since 1988" became "Steelers bulldoze Browns 27-6 to take AFC North lead".
  • A headline written by an AI model was not built: it needs a provider key, and a model can state what no outlet reported.

20.20 curl-impersonate as an extra way to fetch (v0.9.8)

What it is. curl-impersonate (github.com/lwthiker/curl-impersonate, v0.6.1) is a build of curl that sends the TLS handshake, HTTP/2 settings and header order of a real browser. Some sites refuse a plain program by that fingerprint alone, whatever its User-Agent header says. The Dockerfile installs it in /opt/curl-impersonate, downloaded from the release and checked against a pinned SHA-256 for each CPU type (arm64 and amd64). The image grows by about 15 MB.

The switch (Settings → Refresh → Also fetch with curl-impersonate, key fetch.impersonate, default off) and the browser it imitates (fetch.impersonate_profile, default chrome116; also chrome110 to chrome99, edge101, safari15_5, ff117). Off: no curl-impersonate process is ever started. On, it is a second method, not a replacement:

  • Every fetch (feeds, sitemaps, home pages, discovery probes, the reader) first goes the ordinary way. If that returns nothing usable (HTTP 401, 403, 406, 429, 444, 451, 456, 468, 202 or any 5xx; a reset, a TLS failure or a timeout), the same request is repeated once with curl-impersonate. A missing page (404, 410), a name that does not resolve, and a robots.txt refusal are not retried.
  • In the reading pane it is also tried when a page loaded (HTTP 200) but held no article, before the Internet Archive copy.
  • If it also fails, the original result is returned, and the host is not tried with it again for an hour. Up to 4 processes run at once, each limited by the same timeout and 6 MB size cap, and the same per-host spacing applies.
  • robots.txt (when obeyed) is checked before either method. The impersonated request carries the impersonated browser's own User-Agent (Chrome 116 on Windows), because it must match the fingerprint; it is not the value of NA_USER_AGENT. robots.txt files themselves are always fetched the ordinary way.

What to expect. It does not run JavaScript challenges, log in, or get past a block based on the server's address. On a sample of 40 of the 746 no-feed sources that answered HTTP 403, it returned a page for 2 (5%); the rest still returned 403 with a challenge page of 2 to 4 KB. It is most likely to help with sites that refuse by TLS fingerprint and reset or time out the connection.

Code: dao/fetch/impersonate.py (the process, headers, the retry rule) and Fetcher.get in dao/fetch/http.py; the counts are in Fetcher.imp_stats (tried, worked). Tests: tests/test_impersonate.py with a stand-in curl_chrome116.

20.21 Storylines that drifted apart: the 5,389-development storyline and its repair (v0.9.9)

What was wrong. An item in Top / Ongoing, "Healthcare, tariffs, but not Trump impeachment: Democrats chart next two years of power", listed 40 unrelated developments (Hormuz, Sensex, Nigerian politics, weather forecasts, earnings calls). The storyline behind it had 5,389 developments over 10 days; its developments averaged 0.36 similarity to the storyline's centre, and 90% were far from it. The next biggest storylines (100 to 236 developments) averaged 0.56 to 0.60, with 15 to 32% far from the centre.

Why. Developments of one matter more than a day apart were joined into a storyline when they passed the pair test and shared a name or a rare word. Joining moved the whole other storyline across (single linkage), so one loose link pulled in thousands; the only check compared the newcomer with the storyline's first development, which says nothing in a storyline that has already grown mixed. Top / Ongoing and the Storylines tab then ranked a storyline by the plain sum of its developments' coverage, which put the giant first. The page also showed the length of the capped timeline (40), not the real count.

What changed.

  1. Join guard (services/threads.py, ThreadGuard). Two storylines join only if their centres (mean meaning vectors) are at least 0.60 alike and the joined storyline stays cohesive: the mean similarity of its developments to their common centre must stay at least the setting Storylines › Storyline cohesion (default 0.65), however long it gets. For unit vectors that mean is the length of the average vector (|sum| / n), so it is kept as running sums and costs nothing. Both are checked against the running totals as joins accumulate in a pass, so a chain of individually acceptable links cannot add up to a mixed storyline, and a long storyline keeps growing as long as it stays about one thing. (The first version of v0.9.9 used a hard limit of 100 developments; it was replaced by this on 8 Nov 2026, because a fixed length neither stops drift below it nor lets a coherent saga grow.) The same guard applies when two stories of one event merge and bring their storylines along. After Miranda et al. 2018 and Staykovski et al. 2019 (a report joins a cluster by its centroid) and Liu et al. 2017 (Story Forest).
  2. Repair (storyline repair, version 2026-11-08a, ran once at start-up). Every storyline of 8 or more developments whose developments average under the cohesion setting (0.65) in similarity to the centre is re-clustered: pairs at least 0.65 alike that share a name or rare word are linked strongest first, and two groups join only while their centres stay at least 0.60 alike (average linkage) and the joined group's cohesion stays at or above the setting. Each group becomes a storyline; developments that fit nowhere stand alone. Result: 17 storylines split into 2,586; storylines of 20 or more now average 0.72 similarity to their centre (worst 0.64). That run still carried the limit of 100, which split a few coherent long storylines at 100; the cohesion rule replaced it, and on the original 5,390-development storyline it gives groups of up to 394 developments, every one with cohesion at least 0.65. The same start-up run re-applied the earlier name-based repair to the last 7 days (5,095 reports moved into 2,104 new stories, 7,735 developments moved).
  3. Ranking. A storyline in Top / Ongoing, and on the Storylines tab, ranks by its best development plus 0.3 of the others, not their sum, so size alone cannot win.
  4. Watch. After each merge pass, storylines of 8 or more developments updated in the last 3 hours that average under the cohesion setting are re-split; ones that stay whole are not retried until they grow.
  5. Count. A story's detail shows the storyline's real development count and start.

Top / Ongoing before: the giant storyline first, then a 98-development cricket tour, a 212-development NFL "predictions" storyline and others of 64 to 98. After: a Krakow shooting, a Susie Wiles dispute, Israel and Iran, Paraguay's president, the New Zealand election and similar, each of 2 to 59 developments. Backup before the change: na-before-v0910-20261108-2016.dump. Tests: tests/test_threads.py.

Audit of the start-up repair (restored the backup into a scratch database and compared, 8 Nov 2026). The earlier name-based part of the repair moved 5,859 reports (of 567,767) into 2,000 new stories and 8,946 developments between 729 storylines. Measured by meaning (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 on headlines): the stories that lost reports averaged 0.710 similarity to their centre before and 0.726 after, with 1.9% of reports far from it (below 0.4) before and 1.4% after; 72% of the moved reports are closer to the story they went to than to the one they left. For storylines (excluding the giant) the mean went from 0.728 to 0.784 and the far share from 1.6% to 0.3%; the giant became 2,428 storylines (1,864 single developments, 72 of 10 or more). Of 24 random moved developments I judged about 20 right (they left an unrelated storyline and joined one about the same matter or stood alone); the doubtful ones were local items grouped by a generic shape ("rollover crash", "15-year-old shot"). What the repair got wrong: the reports that leave a story were regrouped by shared names, which built template stories: a 129-report "Issues Earnings Results" story of different companies, a 121-report "Earnings Call Transcript" one, a 421-report Diwali-greetings story and two Nigerian 2027-election stories of 105 and 117. Embedding similarity cannot flag these (the titles share a template), and they are not events. The same shape exists in older stories: "Workiva (WK) Q3 2026 Earnings Call Transcript" holds 865 reports from 19 outlets and "Oklo (OKLO) Q3 2026 Earnings Call Transcript" 303. Marking earnings and stock-notice templates as routine (listed, never ranked) and splitting them by company is the follow-up; it is not built.

Setting. Storylines › Storyline cohesion (storylines.cohesion, default 0.65, range 0.4 to 0.9): higher gives tighter, shorter storylines. At 0.70, 41 of 489 live storylines of 8 or more developments would be split; at 0.65, 2.

20.22 Why collection fell behind, and the changes (9 Nov 2026)

Measured. 10,875 feeds were active; at their intervals they needed 8.1 feed checks a second, while the app managed 3 to 4 (8 parallel fetches). 4,451 feeds, all newly discovered, had never been polled and were all due. A merge pass read every story of the last 48 hours (64,568 rows, 210 MB of vectors as text) once per refresh and took 40 to 105 seconds when run next to polling. A few slow feeds (2.5 percent of those sampled) used a third of the fetch time: a 25 s timeout followed by a second try with curl-impersonate. The store gate (one writer of stories) was not a bottleneck: a refresh of 555 feeds waited 31 s in total for it.

  • Polling is a continuous pool of workers (setting Parallel fetches) instead of batches that wait for their slowest feed. The memory governor is asked again every 10 s; it halves the workers above the RAM budget (1 GB) and uses one above 1.25 times the budget.
  • Merging runs next to the polling. A pass starts when reports are waiting and Merge reports at least every (default 2 minutes) has passed since the last one; a backlog of 3,000 or more stories makes the next pass follow at once. The pass reads and compares without a lock; only its writes (merging, joining and splitting storylines, breaking marks, headlines, tags) take the store gate. Lock order is merge lock first, then the gate, everywhere.
  • Slow feeds wait. A timeout or reset on a first try is neither recorded nor retried at once: the feed goes to the end of the refresh's queue. A refusal (403, 429 ...) or server error still goes to curl-impersonate at once; so does a 200 that is not a feed, sitemap or page of links (a challenge or consent page).
  • Backing off. Base interval 30 minutes (was 15). A feed with something new is checked after 0.7 of its interval (shortest a sixth of the base, never under 5 minutes); one with nothing new after 1.5 times (was 1.25), at most 2 hours. Failing feeds double up to 6 hours. No feed is dropped from the schedule.
  • Daily re-analysis (scheduler, once a day, key feed_intervals_at): every working feed gets the interval that matches its last week of articles (about half the usual gap between new articles, within the bounds above; none in the week gives 2 hours). First run, 9 Nov: 3,290 feeds changed (1,475 faster, 1,815 slower). Never-polled feeds start at the base interval.
  • Timing in the log (each Cycle line): fetch_s, stores, store_wait_s, store_hold_s, deferred, retried_ok, unusable_retried, merge_passes, merge_s.

Not done: keeping the 48-hour vectors in memory between passes (option A), which would remove most of the merge pass cost; raising Parallel fetches; vacuuming the story_places, story_topics and reprints tables.

20.23 FlareSolverr as the last way to fetch (9 Nov 2026)

What it is. FlareSolverr (github.com/FlareSolverr/FlareSolverr) opens a page in a real headless Chrome, waits for a JavaScript challenge (Cloudflare's "Just a moment...", DDoS-Guard and similar) to finish, and returns the page and the cookies it earned. It is an optional container (news-anchor-flaresolverr, memory limit 768 MB, about 40 to 90 MB idle) in a compose profile, so the usual redeploy does not start it: docker-compose --profile flaresolverr up -d flaresolverr.

Settings (Settings → Refresh): fetch.flaresolverr (default off), fetch.flaresolverr_url (http://flaresolverr:8191/v1), fetch.flaresolverr_parallel (pages at once, default 1, 1 to 4), fetch.flaresolverr_per_hour (default 60), fetch.flaresolverr_timeout_s (default 60). Each has its own Reset.

Order of attempts. Ordinary request → curl-impersonate (if on) → FlareSolverr (if on). It is tried when the request was refused with HTTP 202, 403, 429 or 503, or when a 200 arrived that is a challenge page (marker text such as "Just a moment", "Checking your browser", "Enable JavaScript and cookies" in a page under 120 KB). Timeouts, resets, 404s and other server errors never go to the browser. Only feeds (all kinds) and the reading pane use it; discovery probes, trust checks and the archive lookup do not (solve=True is passed by the caller).

Performance. A browser takes 5 to 30 s and a few hundred MB, so:

  1. Cookies are reused. After a solve, that site's cookies and the browser's User-Agent are kept (up to 30 minutes, or the cf_clearance expiry) and tried first on the next requests to the site, before the ordinary request. Measured live on a Cloudflare test page: 7.8 s with the browser, 1.6 s on the saved cookies. A site that refuses the cookies (clearance tied to the browser) is marked and solved afresh each time for 24 hours.
  2. Never waits in a feed check. If every slot is busy, the feed check returns "deferred" and the feed goes to the end of the refresh's queue (the same mechanism as slow feeds), so polling of the other feeds is not held up. The final pass waits.
  3. Limits. Pages at once and solves per hour (over all sites, cookie reuse not counted). A site where a solve failed is left alone for 6 hours. A solver that cannot be reached is left alone for 5 minutes.
  4. Only likely challenges. A 200 page goes to the browser only if it looks like a challenge, not every unrecognised page.

What it does not do. CAPTCHAs that need a person, logins, paywalls, blocks on the server's address. An XML response that Chrome wraps in its viewer (<pre>) is unwrapped before it is read. A conditional GET (ETag) is not possible through the browser, so a solved feed is always read in full (articles already stored are not stored twice).

Code: dao/fetch/solver.py (the service call, limits, pauses, challenge markers); Fetcher.get / _reuse / _solved in dao/fetch/http.py; the feed step in IngestService._poll; the reader step in ExtractService. Counts: Fetcher.solver.stats (tried, worked, busy, capped, reused). Tests: tests/test_solver.py.

20.25 Top by period; stories judged by the reports inside the period; the rule that closes a story (12 Nov 2026)

What was wrong. Top had two switches, Fresh and Ongoing. Fresh listed only stories whose first report was under 24 hours old, so a story that kept being reported fell out of the list at the 24-hour mark. Ongoing showed one row per storyline, represented by its newest small development (a 106-development Nvidia storyline appeared as a 2-outlet item). The same 24 hours from the first report also decided whether a new report joined a story: Nvidia's CEO resigned at 14:17 UTC on 12 Nov, but the story it belonged to had a first report stamped 14:13 on 11 Nov (a low-quality outlet's stale date), so the new reports opened separate one-outlet stories instead of joining it.

The lists now. Top has a period bar: Latest (last 3 hours, setting feed.latest_hours), 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 3 days, 7 days. Trending has no bar. Fresh, Ongoing and the Latest tab are gone from the page (the API still has tab=latest, newest first, unused by the page). The URL is ?tab=top&period=7d; keys 1 to 6 pick the period.

Ranking. A story's score is its fair coverage counted from the reports published inside the period: an outlet once, a wire-copy group once, each country's weights adding as log2(1+sum). There is no decay: the period is the freshness. Stories with two or more independent outlets in the period come before single-outlet ones; Breaking stories first in periods of a day or less. Trending is the same score over the last feed.trending_hours (2) only, needing two outlets in them, whenever the story began. The old ongoing burst test (z-score against the day before) is removed with Ongoing. To stay fast, the exact count is made for the 300 stories with the most total coverage among those with a report in the period (the count inside the period cannot exceed the total). Measured on the live database: Latest 0.1 to 0.4 s, 6 h about 0.6 s, 24 h 1 to 2 s, 3 days and 7 days 1 to 3 s; story_sources was made linear (it was quadratic in reprints) and an index on stories(fair) added.

How others do it. Google News names relevance, prominence (how widely a story is covered), freshness and publisher authority, and re-evaluates in short steps; Techmeme orders by the number of links to a story weighted by trusted sources, and by its age; event-detection research tracks a story as a cluster that continues over days and detects bursts against a baseline. This app takes the same two core signals, prominence (fair coverage) and freshness, but makes freshness an explicit window rather than a decay curve, because one decay constant cannot suit both a 3-hour and a 7-day list.

The grouping rule. A report joins a story if it is alike by headline words or meaning and the story is still open: it had a report within the last feed.story_gap_hours (8) and the report is within feed.development_hours (168, a safety limit) of the story's first. A pause of more than 8 hours makes the next report a new development in the same thread. Two stories in the merge pass are one event on the same test over their whole time ranges, not their first reports. A report with exactly the text of a stored one (same headline and lead) is always a reprint of it, however long after: a first version of this change started a new development after a pause, which would let re-posts and spam of one text pile up as new stories; it was undone. The limit of 72 hours I first set had the flaw of the 24-hour rule (a story's age from its first report ends its life while it is still reported); a week makes it rare, and the pause is what ends a story.

Two fixes found from a duplicate (Trump naming Tim Cook for Nvidia, two stories with similarity 0.99). (1) The intake looked for the story of a new report among the 40 newest stories that share any headline word. Of 6,604 stories of the last 3 days that share a word with that report, 141 were newer than its true match, so the match was never looked at. Candidates are now the stories that share the report's rarer words, the ones sharing most first (a test with 60 newer look-alikes fails without it). (2) The merge pass put the two into the 808-outlet Nvidia story through a weak link (similarity 0.67 and one shared rare word); a story of 40 outlets or more (BIG_STORY) now joins another only when their headlines are alike (similarity 0.80 or more).

Times. A date without a time (feeds such as ANI send 00:00:00 UTC) is now taken as the time the report was seen when that is within 36 hours; a time in the future is the time of the check (about 1% of reports are date-only). Existing reports of the last 3 days were corrected once, and the stories of the last 6 hours and all stories of 20 outlets or more were compared again (regroup_version in kv), which joined the day's one-outlet fragments to the Nvidia story and showed it first in Latest, 6 h and 24 h.

Audit of the grouping (scripts/grouping_audit.py, run in a separate container). Of 40 random stories of 12 to 400 reports, none had 20% or more of its report headlines far from the story's centre (the worst 12%): reports are put in the right story. The faults found were about time, not meaning: 865 pairs of a new fragment and a story still being reported that the 24-hour rule kept apart (the Brooklyn building collapse and Nvidia cases), now joined. 20.26 The same news twice in a list; how events, stories and storylines are told apart (13 Nov 2026)

What was wrong. Top 7 days showed two rows for the Nvidia CEO scandal. The cause was our own rule: a story takes reports for 48 hours after its first report; the first report after that starts a second story of the same storyline. Both stories were still receiving reports, so both ranked, and a list showed each as a row. Other cases found while checking: a story for the same event that the merge had not joined (three stories for the death of Dan Hicks), and the merge pass refusing to join two established stories unless they were 0.95 alike.

The three levels. (1) A report is one article. (2) A story is the reports of one event, in a time window: joined by headline words or meaning, open while reports keep coming (8-hour pause or 48 hours ends it), merged with a similar story only when the headlines also name the same things (anchors: people, teams, places) and give no different casualty count. (3) A storyline (thread) is the stories that belong together over days, joined by a shared name or rare word and kept only while their centres stay alike. A list shows stories: a new development (the resignation, the new nominee) is its own row, the storyline links them in the story page.

How others do it. News-stream clustering systems work in two steps. newsLens (Laban and Hearst, 2017) first finds topics inside a short batch, then links the topics of different batches into long stories; Miranda et al. (EMNLP 2018) and the BNP Paribas system built on newsLens (2020) link a batch's topics to earlier ones by centroid similarity and use TF-IDF sparse vectors with entities, which beat dense vectors for one language (Staykovski et al., 2019); Event Registry (Leban et al.) clusters per language and links across languages. Our short-window step is the story, the linking step is the storyline. What none of them leaves to chance is the cut between batches: a batch boundary must not become two rows.

Fixes. (a) Chains. A story begun by the first report after the length limit, while a story of ten outlets or more is still being reported, and which says the same thing (three headline words and a name in common, or similarity 0.80), is a continuation: stories.chain_id points to the first story and a list shows the chain as one row, counted over all parts (stories.link_chains, run after each merge pass). Anything less stays two rows: a check of every chain on the live data first showed that a looser rule (same storyline, similarity 0.5) joined different events (a Kenyan army bomb find with an army recruitment notice; two nations'' football squads), so the rule was tightened to the cut itself. (b) Duplicate check when a list is made (services/dedupe.py): a row is dropped if a higher row has similarity 0.82 or more, overlaps it in time (within the story gap), shares a third of its rare headline words, gives no different casualty count and names no different things. Tested to keep apart: Gaza death toll 74,640 and four killed in a sand collapse (similarity 0.97); a fire killing 12 and one killing 3; the same talks four days apart. (c) The merge pass joins two established stories (40 outlets each) from similarity 0.90, not 0.95.

Checks. scripts/list_audit.py reads every Top period and Trending as a reader gets them and reports pairs of the same news; on the live data it found 11 pairs before the fixes and 1 to 2 (angle stories) after. The distinct events that stay separate were also listed: Tim Cook named for Nvidia vs the resignation, two US talks with Putin four days apart, COP31 opening vs a bilateral talk at COP31.

Found on the way. The app was killed by the kernel for memory (container limit 1.5 GB) every few minutes after the last regrouping, and each restart began the six-day retag again, so it never finished; the retag was run once in a separate container. The database container had only 64 MB of /dev/shm, so some parallel queries failed with "No space left on device"; it now has 256 MB.

20.27 Top and Trending as Fresh, Ongoing and Aggregated (13 Nov 2026)

Why. A period bar (Latest, 6 h ... 7 days) made the reader choose a window and still showed one huge event for days. Ranking only by size keeps old giants on top; ranking only by time fills the list with the 98% of stories that have one or two outlets (63,376 stories began in 24 hours; 1,523 had three outlets or more, 159 ten or more, 10 fifty or more). Early counts do not tell which story will be big: of stories that ended with ten outlets or more, only 11% had five outlets within an hour, and a trust-weighted count did no better. So the lists are split by what a reader wants.

The views (the same three in Top and in Trending; keys 1, 2, 3). Fresh: stories whose event was first reported in the last 24 hours (setting feed.fresh_hours): a new event, or a new development of a storyline that had been quiet (stories are developments, and a story cut by the length limit counts from its first part). Ongoing: stories that began earlier and are still being reported in the last 24 hours. Aggregated (default): both. In Top the two lists are fused by rank (reciprocal rank fusion, k = 3: a story scores 1 / (3 + its place in its own list)), because a giant's coverage and a new story's are not on one scale; the first of each share the top, then the seconds. In Trending the surge is on one scale, so the stories are sorted. Each side is read for its own best 120 candidates, so the giants cannot push every fresh story out of the candidates. A row in Aggregated says Fresh or Ongoing.

Top ranking. The fair coverage of 20.25 (an outlet once, a wire-copy group once, each country adding as log2(1 + weight)) of the reports of the last 24 hours, each outlet counted by when it joined: (1 + age / T)^-g, T = 6 hours, g = 2 (settings feed.decay_hours, feed.decay_gravity). An outlet that joined 6 hours ago counts for a quarter, one from 12 hours ago for a ninth. Trending ranking. (outlets that joined in the last 2 hours - their usual number per 2 hours, read from the day before) / sqrt(usual + 1), at least 3 outlets; a story that began in the window has no usual number. The same 100 outlets an hour for two days is not a surge. In both, stories with enough outlets come before the rest; a story that repeats a better one (similarity 0.82, overlapping in time, sharing rare words and names, no different casualty count) is dropped (services/dedupe.py).

What was taken out, and why. The replay (scripts/replay_lists.py) compared designs; features that did not show a gain, or could not be measured, were removed: seen/read marking and its server table (no way to show it helped; opened stories fell out of sight), the "New: ..." phrases and the new-development detector (headline words such as "upon" or "3" were noise; the boost did not list big stories earlier in Latest), the one-storyline penalty (changed none of the numbers: the first ten rows already came from about ten storylines), the catch-up card and new-stories pill, the Breaking pin and boost (five outlets within half an hour is a low bar; small stories sat above the biggest news; the Breaking label stays but does not rank), and the period bar. Kept: decay (found big stories sooner), the surge for Trending (the largest gain), the candidate rule for stories begun inside the window, chains (an event cut by the length limit is one row), and the duplicate check.

Replay (16 snapshots over two days, 64 stories that finally had 50 outlets or more, first ten rows of each list). Share of the big stories listed within 24 hours of their first report: before (24 hours of coverage, no decay) 14%, Aggregated 30%, Fresh 45%, Trending 55%; within 6 hours: 6%, 14%, 16%, 34%; never listed: 86%, 70%, 55%, 45%. The lists change more (share of the first ten rows not in the previous snapshot's: 16% before, 21% Aggregated, 35% Fresh, 87% Trending). Decay T = 12 h, g = 1.5 was tried: Aggregated 27% / 11%, Fresh 42% / 11%, so T = 6, g = 2 stays. Ten rows cannot hold 64 stories, and "big" is known in hindsight, so read the differences. The Nvidia check (replay at every hour of 12 Nov, aggregated list): the resignation story was 2nd at 13:00 and 1st at 15:00 and 16:00; the story that gathered the later reports was 6th at 13:00, 5th at 16:00 and 1st from 17:00 to the end; neither was ever out of the first six.

Distinct events inside a big storyline (14 Nov 2026). The "Trump names Tim Cook for Nvidia" story vanished from the Nvidia storyline. Cause, shown with the merge code on three stories: the merge pass joins stories by chains of pairwise links and checked the combined group only at 0.62, so a small story (5 outlets) that was 0.85 to the Tim Cook story (30 outlets, 0.71 to the big one) and 0.82 to the big story (300 outlets) carried the Tim Cook story into it; without the small story nothing merged. Now a group takes in another group only if the other group's largest story is itself at least 0.80 to the big group's centre (0.90 if it is a big story too). The storyline's timeline lists stories, so a merged story had no entry and the story that holds it was hidden among 18 newer reports of one or two outlets; the timeline now lists the developments with three outlets or more (all of them in a storyline of eight or fewer), and counts the rest ("and 59 smaller reports").

Parts of a story. A story can hold a distinct part (services/subevents.py): the words that several outlets use and most of the story's reports do not (the theme words, used by 40% or more of the reports, are left out); words used by the same reports are one part. The story page lists them under "Within this story" with a headline and the outlets and reports behind it. On the Nvidia story it lists, among others, "Trump calls for Apple ex-CEO Tim Cook to replace Huang" (81 outlets) and "Nvidia names CFO Colette Kress as interim" (73). Some single-word parts are noisy.

One row for one storyline. In Top and Trending, developments of one storyline that are all in the list become one row that opens ("Show 2 more developments of this storyline in this list"), when they are at least 0.65 alike in meaning (a storyline is wider than an event: the Ukraine storyline also holds the Hormuz talks, at 0.49) and have the outlets the list needs. Nothing is dropped. The two Nvidia stories (0.75 alike) are one row.

Not solved. (1) An event split over several small stories is ranked by each part (a pooled score needs a safe test for "same event"; the tests tried either hid distinct events or did nothing). (2) Stories that the earlier merge passes joined are not split again: the Tim Cook story stays inside the big Nvidia story and shows as a part of it. (3) Parts are found from headlines only; words such as "chip" or "affair" make a broad part.

Also. Lists are reused for 20 seconds. The database container now has 768 MB (512 MB was killed by the kernel when the replay and the app ran queries at the same time) and parallel queries are off.

20.28 Latest tab and clean-up of one-outlet stories (14 Nov 2026)

Why. Top and Trending depend on how many outlets cover a story, so a fresh event that only a few outlets have picked up cannot show there yet. Latest lists new news, but not raw: of the 710,000 stories stored, 91% were reported by one outlet, and most of those are advertisements, spam, or posts nobody else carried.

Latest. A story is listed when it is confirmed: latest.min_outlets (3) different outlets reported it, counted so that an outlet is one, outlets that printed the same wire text are one, and reports published within the same latest.spread_minutes (3) minutes are one (posts placed at one moment are how spam networks and scheduled syndication look; the same idea as the Breaking rule). A story reported by latest.sure_outlets (10) different outlets is listed without the timing test: many outlets writing their own text of one event at once is how a big breaking story looks. The list is ordered by qualified_at, the time of the report that completed the confirmation, newest first, within latest.hours (24). A headline that is an advertisement (services/spam.py: sponsored posts, promo and coupon codes, shopping-event deal lists, "buy now") is never listed; a repeat of an event already listed (same check as Top) is dropped; social posts alone and routine items (scoreboards, filings) cannot qualify. Candidates come from stories that have enough independent outlets and were updated inside the window; the timing is read from the articles (services via list_stories, tab latest).

Why these rules. One-outlet stories are the main source of spam and advertisements; a count of different outlets with a spread in time is the cheap form of corroboration used by news-event detectors (a story is an event when several independent sources report it) and the timing test follows the usual sign of coordinated posting, many posts at one instant. A wire story that 300 stations print still counts once, so only independent reporting confirms a story. The cost: a real event that only one outlet has reported (a local fire) is not in Latest until a second and third outlet report it; it stays stored for the wait below.

Clean-up. A story that only one outlet reported (outlets ≤ 1 and independent outlets ≤ 1), that is in no storyline with a story of two outlets or more, that is not saved and not social, and whose last report is older than storage.purge_wait_hours (84 h) is deleted with its articles and reprints; so is a story with an advertisement's headline and independent outlets ≤ 1. The wait is never shorter than refresh.max_item_age_hours + 12 h: a feed keeps showing an item for days, and an item deleted while still in its feed would be stored again at the next check. Runs with the storage check (every 10 minutes, at most 45 seconds per run, 4,000 stories per batch). Counters are kept in kv purge_stats and shown on Status. Setting: storage.purge_uncorroborated (on). Deleted rows free space inside the tables for new rows; the files shrink only after a VACUUM FULL.

Effects to know. Explore and Status count the articles that are kept, so their totals now describe corroborated reporting. A story picked up by a second outlet after its wait is a new story (the first report is gone). Sources, publishers, feeds and the trust crawl do not change.

Activity scaling in Top (14 Nov 2026). The Jensen Huang resignation story stayed at #6 in Aggregated with no outlet joining it for six hours. Two reasons: the score counts each outlet of the last day with a decay weight (an outlet that joined 20 hours ago still counts for about 5%), and many such outlets add up; and Aggregated merges the Fresh and Ongoing lists by rank only (reciprocal rank fusion), so the third-best Ongoing story keeps its share of the list however small its score. Now an ongoing story (begun more than a day ago) is multiplied by n / (n + c), n = outlets that joined it in feed.active_hours (6), c = feed.active_scale (12 by default; 6 first): none counts for nothing, 12 for a half, 60 for 83%; the same factor weights its share in the rank fusion. Fresh stories are not scaled; Trending already measures arrivals. A hard floor (a minimum number of recent outlets, removing the story) was tried first and replaced: it gave the same discovery and less repetition gain. Replay of three days (24 snapshots, 49 big stories, Aggregated): found within 24 hours 63% with no scaling, 63% with a hard floor of 3, 63% with c = 3, 69% with c = 6 and 69% with c = 12; places held for over 24 hours 12%, 10%, 7%, 5%, 3%; top-10 places with no outlet joined in 6 hours 1%, 0%, 0%, 0%, 0%; churn 26%, 27%, 29%, 31%, 33%; share of Ongoing in the first ten 50%, 50%, 45%, 39%, 33%. The second resignation story sank gradually (from #5 to #16 with c = 6 at 13 Nov 22:00, to #84 by 14 Nov 01:00) where it had stayed at #5 to #6 without scaling; Outlook, still gaining outlets, was unaffected (#1 to #3).

Scale 12 against 6, and scaling Fresh (14 Nov 2026). A second replay (24 snapshots, 50 big stories, Aggregated) with scale 0 / 6 / 12: found within 3 hours 6 / 6 / 8%, within 6 hours 18 / 20 / 22%, within 24 hours 62 / 68 / 68%, places held over 24 hours 12 / 5 / 3%, churn 26 / 31 / 33%, Ongoing share of the first ten 50 / 38 / 33%. The differences between 6 and 12 are about one story of fifty; 12 was chosen as the default because it repeats least. Scaling Fresh stories the same way (c = 3, 6, 12 on top of 6 or 12 for Ongoing) found the same big stories in the Fresh view (72% within a day) and slightly fewer in Aggregated (64 to 66% against 68%), with more churn: a story first reported within the day cannot repeat for more than a day, so there is nothing for the scaling to remove. Not adopted; the parameter (ranking.Params.fresh_soft) stays at 0.

Live lists. The News page refetches every list when the server announces new stories (debounced 1.5 s), when its event connection comes back after a break, when the tab becomes visible again, and every 60 seconds while visible; the server reuses a list for 20 seconds. Explore counts the articles published in its period (7 days by default) from sources that published any; Status counts everything stored and every known source.

Status page wording. A source is an outlet in the registry (10,800), each with feeds; websites seen (20,000) are all sites that appeared in the GDELT and Google News streams and searches, of which a website becomes a candidate after a second sighting and a source after a working feed is found; the page said "publishers" for both and now says "websites seen" for the second.
